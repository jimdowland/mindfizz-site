Behaviour over time chart

What this shows

How a key metric moved over time, with the decisions and events that surrounded it. The aim is to make the relationship between actions and outcomes visible, without overclaiming that one caused the other.

Where this works well

Board and leadership reviews where performance has changed and the reasons are disputed. Evaluation reporting where funders need to understand context, not just results.

Limitations to be aware of

Depends on having reliable longitudinal data. The choice of which events to annotate shapes the story, that selection needs to be made carefully and transparently. Works best when the data period is long enough to show a meaningful pattern.

Decision and action timeline

What this shows

The gap between when decisions are made and when results become visible. It separates the decision line from the action line, making it possible to see that work was underway even while performance continued to fall.

Where this works well

Situations where slow results are being misread as programme failure. Useful for boards, funders or oversight bodies who need to understand why investment didn't produce immediate improvement. Particularly effective when there is pressure to intervene before an intervention has had time to work.

Limitations to be aware of

Requires enough documented history to reconstruct the sequence accurately. The timeline explains what happened, it doesn't predict when results will arrive.

Causal loop diagram

What this shows

How different parts of a system interact over time. Rather than pointing to a single cause, it maps the feedback loops that produced a pattern of behaviour: in this case, why performance declined and what eventually stabilised it.

Where this works well

Multi-factor situations where single cause explanations have already proved insufficient. Strategy and planning conversations where understanding reinforcing and balancing loops matters. Most effective when a practitioner is present to guide the reading.

Limitations to be aware of

Complex for a general audience. The notation, positive and negative relationships, loop labels and delay markers, is unfamiliar to most audiences without an introduction. Not suitable as a standalone leave-behind.

Triangle comparison (240k vs 9k)

What this shows

A single comparison made impossible to dismiss. The triangle device encodes magnitude without requiring the reader to interpret a chart, the scale differential is visible before the numbers are read.

Where this works well

Opening or anchoring a policy argument where the scale of a problem needs to land immediately. Useful when the audience already knows the situation but hasn't felt its weight. Effective with non-specialist audiences, media-facing materials and funder briefings where attention is short.

Limitations to be aware of

A device this stark invites challenge — the figures need to be defensible and sourced. It communicates scale but not cause, so it needs to be followed by something that explains why the gap exists. Used in isolation it can feel polemical rather than analytical.

Problem framing

What this shows

A structured argument built from short statements, each one advancing the case, the final one a conclusion. No charts, no diagrams. The visual device is the sequence.

Where this works well

Setting up the terms of a debate before evidence is introduced. Useful when the audience holds a view that needs to be reframed. Works well as a standalone slide in a live presentation where the speaker controls the pace. Each statement can be held on-screen before the next appears.

Limitations to be aware of

Depends entirely on the quality of the writing. Takes a clear position, which is appropriate in advocacy contexts but needs to be appropriate to the audience and setting. Does not work as a leave-behind without supporting material.

Permissions granted vs homes completed

What this shows

The gap between planning permission and actual construction, and the reasons that gap persists. The area chart makes the structural divergence visible over time; the explanatory panel on the right explains what the gap means.

Where this works well

Policy and advocacy contexts where a common assumption, in this case, that planning is the barrier to housebuilding, needs to be tested against evidence. Useful when the audience includes people with competing explanations for the same problem.

Limitations to be aware of

Requires reliable longitudinal data from a credible source. The chart is only as strong as the figures behind it. The slide takes a position rather than simply presenting data. That is sometimes what is needed.

Access determined at design stage

What this shows

A two-column structure that maps the conditions shaping access against the consequences of ignoring them. The parallel layout makes the relationship between cause and effect visible without requiring a diagram.

Where this works well

Opening or reframing a conversation where the audience understands the problem in general terms but hasn't connected it to their own programme design decisions. Effective with policy, funder and partner audiences where the case for early intervention needs to be made before any specific risks are discussed. Works well as a standalone slide in a live presentation where the speaker can hold each line before moving on.

Limitations to be aware of

Works less well as a leave-behind without supporting context.

Risk matrix

What this shows

A severity / likelihood matrix that positions specific access risks by their priority for action. Rather than listing risks equally, it forces a distinction between what needs to be addressed urgently, what should be monitored, and what can be deprioritised.

Where this works well

Programme design and review stages where a team needs to move from identifying risks to deciding which ones to act on first. Useful in workshop settings where the positioning of items on the matrix can be discussed and adjusted. Effective with technically informed audiences who need to prioritise limited resources across multiple risk areas.

Limitations to be aware of

The severity and likelihood assessments are judgements, not measurements. The matrix is only as reliable as the evidence and expertise behind those judgements.

Programme lifecycle diagram

What this shows

Where advisory input can be integrated across the full programme. The diagram makes visible something that is often assumed but rarely stated: that the greatest impact comes from early engagement.

Where this works well

Conversations with funders, commissioners or senior leadership about when and how to bring in external advisory support. Useful for making the case for upstream engagement rather than end-stage review.

Limitations to be aware of

The lifecycle model is a simplification, real programmes rarely move through stages. The diagram can imply more predictability than exists in practice.
