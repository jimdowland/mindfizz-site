We often treat data as neutral and objective, like a mirror of reality. But how we choose to present data is often a rhetorical decision. Choices mean that the same number can tell radically different stories.

Before we create a single chart we have already decided what measures to use, which period to show and what to compare and what to emphasise.

These choices can shape the reader’s interpretation. Though this doesn’t automatically mean that the evidence is being distorted, these decisions influence what the reader focuses on, which comparisons they choose to make and what conclusions they come to.

## One dataset. Four treatments.

In this example we take one data set, the number of council houses built in the UK, and show how decision around selection can influence interpretation.

### Explore

This shows the complete picture and is ideal if your intention is for the reader to interrogate the data. In this case, the headline story is clear, a dramatic drop in build from the post war high in the mid 1950s (the Macmillan race to build) – to the dramatic drop off in the early 80s (The housing act 1980). This version demonstrates scale and history but the wider context resists a single, simple explanation

### Direct attention

By limiting the time frame we tell the reader exactly what we want them to focus on. In the example we restrict our attention to the period surrounding 1980. We want the user to see the cliff edge and ask themselves why. We have moved towards a political or historical critique by guiding the reader to a specific inflection point.

### Compare

The contrast is stark and the implication is hard to ignore. We have removed all other context and nuance and simply provide a comparison of before and after. We are very explicitly pointing the reader to the fall off in a way that is very easy to understand. The intention here could be to trigger an emotional or a policy reaction

### Emphasise.

Here we present one carefully selected statistic. This is designed for maximum reaction and retention – it could be used on a hero slide, a section opener or on social media.

## When presentation becomes persuasion

A detailed chart invite investigation. A carefully cropped trend can highlight a dramatic period. A two-year comparison can make a particular difference memorable. A single number can become a powerful statement.

The data is unchanged, but its rhetorical function is not. These choices and their effects should be understood by the designer, who should take responsibility for them.

So when does this become misleading? Good data visualisation is not always neutral, but it should always be defensible. That means asking whether the amount of selection and the degree of emphasis suit the purpose, and whether enough context has been preserved.
