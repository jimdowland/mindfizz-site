We’ve noticed a pattern over the years in the development of complex reports and presentations.

The project often starts with a clear purpose, and a request for contributions; relevant examples, supporting evidence and background material.

What comes back is a range of assets in various formats and levels of detail  – often contributed in an ever-expanding email trail with dozens of attachments. Examples overlap. Duplicates creep in.

As the process progresses and the presentation day approaches this density of information increases as the fear of omitting a vital piece of evidence or the internal politics of including everything begins bear down on the team members responsible for delivery. We call this the pressure pattern and we’ve captured it in the visual above.

Early on the pressures are competing priorities and conflicting objectives. In the later stages they’ve become evolving requirements, late-stage additions a panicked, final rounds of amendments. At this point the pressure is at its peak.

## When information becomes a design problem

As the presentation comes together contributors compete for inclusion. Often with different tones of voice, structure and levels of detail. And in later stages, some defensive additions are made to try and ‘cover off’ various contingencies.

This results in a dense piece of communication which is then regarded as a design problem. The priority them becomes: how to make the slides look calm and on brand and not like a wall of text?  Decisions of hierarchy, purpose and priority that were deferred, have now become problems for the layouts to solve.

By this point it can be very hard to refocus – and the audience, communication, context of delivery and even the purpose can be lost in the sheer volume of content.

## The value of a critical friend

This is why early structural and editorial decisions matter.

These decisions will inevitably evolve as the process develops.  New evidence may change the core argument, and allowing for a range of perspectives can enhance the final product. We’ve found that not everything can be fixed using a defined structure at the outset, flexibility must be retained and the structure should adapt as the content develops.

And this is where having a critical friend can be useful.

Someone working alongside the core team, but also with a little distance can ask questions that maybe more difficult to ask from inside the team.

- What is the audience being asked to understand or decide?
- Which example strengthen the argument?
- Are some points overly defensive?
- Is there over-explanation?
- Could a paragraph be replaced with a diagram?
- And should some of the detail stay out of the main presentation and provided as supporting material instead?

Ultimately, the critical friend brings structure to the content the subject experts provide. The earlier we ask questions about purpose, priority and structure, the less we need visual design to come to the rescue later.
