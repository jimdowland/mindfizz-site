We’re interested in the systems thinking, specifically the visual tools that have come out of it, and how we can adapt them for our practice.

As an exercise we took a common scenario (illustrative) to see if we could use these tools to help explain not just what happened and when, but also why

We kept the scenario very simple so we could focus on the visuals, A key metric, customer satisfaction (CSAT), dropped and, after some intervention, improved.

## Visual: Behaviour over time (What changed)

Establishes the observed pattern, its magnitude and duration.

A simple line chart shows the movement of CSAT over time, clearly highlighting the drop in performance. We’ve also added some annotations for context. By overlaying key events on the metric, we show where changes line up with events, without claiming one caused the other.

## Visual: Decision and action timeline (what happened along the way)

Places decisions, interventions and outcomes in sequence, making potential delays visible.

The purpose of this is to show that gap between the point where a decision is made and when the result becomes visible. Often there is a lag between decisions and outcomes which might be mistaken as a programme failure.

## Visual: Causal loop diagram (CLD) (What might explain the pattern)

  <figcaption>Illustrative scenario · <a href="/assets/img/insights/visualising-what-happened-3.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View diagram at full size ↗</a></figcaption>
</figure>

This visual is the one that is most associated with systems thinking. The examples we looked at were very academic and visually complex. We wondered if it would be possible to simplify the structure to repurpose it for a non-academic audience.

The first task was to identify the most significant variables and relationships – this involves some editorial judgement.

CLDs often complex and intricate, and difficult and time consuming to understand.

We reduced ours to two loops 1 balancing and 1 reinforcing and were influenced by transport maps which have been specifically designed to be interpreted quickly.

CLDs have a formalised set of notations, we evaluated these for use outside of an academic context, but kept the polarity icons, which indicate if two variables connected by an arrow both move together (+) or opposite directions (-)

CLDs provide a way of representing proposed relationships, and can help teams discuss relationships, but the relationships need supporting evidence.

## What we learned

Our key takeaway from this exercise is that each visual added a different layer of understanding. The behaviour-over-time chart showed the change, the timeline helped us understand the sequence of events, and the causal diagram offered a way to explore the relationships behind them.

On their own, none gave a complete explanation, and any claim of cause still need evidence to support it. But used together these visuals helped us move beyond reporting performance to understanding what might be driving it.
